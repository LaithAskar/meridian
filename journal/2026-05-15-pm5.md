## Explanation

### Event loop ordering

The central loop is:

```
for each trading day t:
    1. apply pending fills from t-1 at t's open prices   ← fills.fill_order()
    2. mark portfolio to t's close prices
    3. snapshot (equity, cash, num_positions)
    4. call strategy.on_bar(t, bars_t, portfolio)  → new orders
    5. store new orders as pending for t+1
```

The ordering of steps 1–4 is the most consequential design choice. By applying fills **before** snapshotting and **before** calling `on_bar`, the strategy sees the post-fill portfolio on every call. This matters for sizing: if the strategy asks "how many positions do I have?", it sees the filled state, not the pre-fill state. This matches what a live strategy would observe.

Fills use next-bar-open (from `fills.py`) — the strategy cannot fill at the price that triggered the signal, which is the standard anti-look-ahead convention.

### Portfolio accounting

`_apply_fill` is the mutation kernel. For a **buy**: cash decreases by `qty × fill_price`; the position is created (new) or updated via weighted average cost basis (additive buy). For a **sell**: qty is silently capped at held qty (no shorts); cash increases; a closed-trade dict is returned capturing entry avg-cost and exit fill-price.

**Why weighted average cost?** FIFO would require a lot queue per symbol. Weighted avg is simpler, identical to FIFO for single-entry positions (which is the normal case with the equal-weight, one-entry-per-symbol strategy design), and is the standard in most academic backtesting literature.

**Why cap sells at held qty rather than reject the order?** Capping is friendlier to strategy bugs — a strategy that accidentally sends a 2× oversell just closes the position cleanly rather than crashing. The P&L is honest because the trade record captures the actual qty closed.

### BacktestResult shape

Two DataFrames are returned:

1. `equity_curve` — one row per trading day; columns: equity (marked to market at close), cash, num_positions. The consumer (metrics.py) will call `equity_curve["equity"].pct_change().dropna()` to get daily returns for Sharpe.

2. `trades` — one row per closed/partially-closed position; columns include entry_price (avg cost basis at exit time), exit_price, pnl, holding_days. This is the input to `hit_rate()` and `avg_win_loss()` in metrics.py.

**Why record trades at the sell side?** Because that is when P&L is realized and knowable. Recording at buy time would require matching buys to sells after the fact. Capturing avg_cost at the moment of sell is equivalent and avoids a post-processing join.

### _bars_at efficiency

`bars.xs(ts, level="date")` is O(log n) on a sorted MultiIndex and returns a single-level (symbol) DataFrame. Converting to `{symbol: Series}` gives the strategy a clean dict-access interface rather than requiring MultiIndex lookups inside the hot loop.

### Alternatives rejected

- **Vectorized event loop (shift-based):** A pure-pandas approach would shift the buy/sell signals forward by one bar using `df.shift(1)` and compute fill prices as `open.shift(-1)`. This is faster but completely opaque — the fill-timing invariant is implicit in an index offset, which is a common source of look-ahead bugs. The explicit row-by-row loop makes the next-bar-open constraint impossible to accidentally violate.
- **Storing full positions in the snapshot:** Keeping `dict[symbol, Position]` per timestamp would consume `O(universe × days)` memory (~100 × 3500 = 350k rows of dicts). We only need the aggregate count for metrics; full positions can be reconstructed from the trades log if needed.
